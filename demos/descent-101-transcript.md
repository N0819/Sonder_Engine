# The Descent

A hundred and one beats through the real Sonder pipeline, played straight:
every player line was written blind to the state, every other word on this
page is the engine's.

Aurel Voss is a maintenance engineer. Dr Sarah Moon is a researcher. A
Euclid-class anomaly has breached containment somewhere else in Site-17, the
renovated shelter route is cut off, and the pair of them go down the old
auxiliary lift instead.

The thing that hunts them is a Charter creature, not a scripted monster. Its
card says `hearing: false` and `scent: true`: it is DEAF, and it tracks
exhaled carbon dioxide. It decides where to walk from its own scent map on
its own schedule -- when it closes, nothing told it to.

Neither character is ever told what it hunts by. Sarah works it out at beat
90 from what she has seen of it, and the last act is built on her deduction.

The run was played to find engine defects and it found sixteen. Several are
visible here as beats that read slightly wrong; they are catalogued in
`docs/UNBUILT.md` 1.151-1.158.

### 0. _(opening)_

**CRACK--BOOM**

Site-17's klaxon blared throughout the facility. A containment breach. The PA regularly transmitted a message, one Sarah Moon had heard more times than she'd like.

*"Attention All Site Staff: Code Yellow. The site is experiencing a Euclid-class containment breach. Please make your way to the nearest shelter. Site security will guide you. Mobile Task Force Epsilon-11 is on-site and responding to the incident. This message will repeat until the containment breach is resolved."* 

*Yes, yes. Run and run.* 

This is why Sarah Moon wore loafers and not heels - Too many of the staff tripped and died from heels. Her footsteps carried her at a steady, quick pace through towards the shelter. Smoke filled the area, a result of the escaped anomaly's rampage. Sarah Moon simply regretted that she could not grab snacks from her office before she had to get to the shelter.

At the end of the hallway, the red lights faded. This section still had power, and two blue lights illuminated the elevator down into the shelter. It was... quiet. Not anomalously quiet, where Sarah Moon was usually was less populated, and the shelter here was an older model. Still safe, but not with some of the luxuries of the one that received renovations. She assumed most of her coworkers had gone there. But there, in the elevator, was a figure. The finger on the button to descend.

Sarah felt her heart skip a beat. That was... not good. She would be safe in the elevator, but not here, the anomaly was in between her and the other shelter. It took precious minutes for the elevator to descend and come back up. She found herself speaking before her mind could catch up.

"Ah--!" The hurried, fearful puff of air came from the woman, completely uncharacteristic of the researcher. She tempered it quickly, but the urgency was still there in her tone. "Ex-- Excuse me! Please, hold the elevator!" Sarah Moon called, moving faster than she had before towards the lift.

There was no shame in the request - Dr. Sarah Moon was something now she rarely was. Scared. And the only security was the elevator to the shelter, the figure inside being the person who would decide if she lived, or died.

### 1. I get my hand across the door track before she finishes the sentence — palm flat on the rubber edge so the interlock reads an obstruction and holds. The doors shudder against my arm and stay open. "In. Come on." I keep my arm where it is and step back off the panel with the other foot, making room. There is plenty of it; this car is rated for twelve and it is holding one tired engineer and a tool belt. Past her shoulder the corridor is going grey with smoke, and I do not like how quiet it is under the klaxon. "I'm Voss. Facilities — I keep this thing running. Get in and I'll take us down to sub-three."

She stepped through the doorway at a brisk pace, smoothing the front of her white lab coat with one hand as her stride settled. Straight silvery-blue hair fell past her hips over a dark collared shirt and tailored pencil skirt. Near the heavy accordion gate, she came to a halt with an invariant, deadpan neutral expression, her deep violet gaze level and unblinking. "Doctor Sarah Moon, Psychology. Do not close the doors. The primary bulkhead behind me has fully sealed. I am boarding now."

Under my palm, the door track pressed back with steady, mechanical weight. The rubber edge bit into my skin as the interlock strained against the hold, but the frame stayed open.

### 2. "Doors," I say, and take my arm off the track. They come together properly this time, the rubber seals kissing, and the corridor goes away behind them. I put the flat of my hand on the sub-three button and hold it until the relay under the panel clunks over. "Sub-three. Ninety seconds, give or take." The car takes up the load and starts down. I lean my shoulder blades against the back wall and let out a breath I have apparently been holding since the klaxon started. "Psychology," I say, without any particular tone. "You were on eleven, then. That's a long way to come on foot with a Euclid loose."

Sarah stepped back half a pace into the cab, resting both arms at her sides as she let the momentum of the lift settle underfoot. She spoke in a composed voice: "Sub-level eleven, yes. Section D bulkheads engaged thirty seconds after the siren commenced, which necessitated a detour through maintenance conduit four. Ninety seconds is an acceptable transit duration. Thank you for holding the elevator, Mister Voss." Standing centered in the cab, she smoothed down her cuffs at her sides.

### 3. "Eleven's a long way up. You should have—" I do not finish it, because the floor drops out from under the word. Not far. A hand's width, maybe, the kind of drop you feel in your knees before your ears catch up, and then the car catches itself hard enough that my teeth click. The hum I have listened to for eleven years goes out of the walls. I get a hand on the rail and the other on the panel, and I do not look at her yet, because I am listening. That is the guide shoe. That is not the guide shoe. "Hold on to something."

The steel rail bit into my knuckles as my boots held against the drop, my other palm flattened against the dead casing of the panel. The overheads died with the hum, pitching the car into total dark where nothing showed through the seams of the door.

Behind me, Sarah widened her stance slightly. She clamped both hands onto the wall rail, bracing her shoulders back against the diamond plate.

"I have secured a hold. Did the hoist cable slip, or was that a secondary brake engagement?" she said, her voice clinical and steady.

### 4. Dark. Properly dark, the kind you only get down here where there are no windows to leak anything. I do not answer her, not yet. I find the emergency lamp on my belt by feel and thumb it on — a hard white cone, unkind to look at — and put it first on the ceiling hatch, then on the door seam, then on the indicator strip above the panel, which is dead. "Neither," I say. "Cable slip you'd feel twice and we'd still be moving. Brake bites hard and holds hard. That was the drive letting go and the safeties catching us." I put the beam on the seam again. "We're between floors. And the power's not browned out, Doctor, it's gone. Nothing on this panel has any right to be dark." I get my fingers into the door seam and pull, testing what the doors will give me.

The doors gave nothing back. The rubber seam pinched hard against my knuckles as I leaned in, hauling with my full weight, but the plates stayed frozen in the sill. The stark glare of the utility lamp caught the heavy accordion gate and the threshold, throwing harsh reflections off the locked frame before thinning to half-light across the industrial brass and steel control box.

Within arm's reach on my left, Sarah spoke in a deadpan, clinical monotone: "Total failure of both primary and auxiliary feeds indicates either structural severance on the upper decks or automated containment quarantine." She released one hand from the rail while keeping her stance braced, looking upward toward the illuminated door seam and the ceiling hatch. Her voice carried an inquiring edge, though the delivery remained clinical: "If the cab is arrested between landing zones, the mechanical interlock will resist manual separation until the clutch is tripped. Do you require manual assistance, or is there a release tool on your belt?"

### 5. "There's a release tool on my belt, Doctor, because I am the release tool." I put the lamp in my teeth, get the pry bar off the belt and set the flat of it into the seam at waist height, where the interlock clutch sits on a Mark IV car. Then I lean on it. It goes in an inch, and I feel the clutch let go with a sound like a knuckle cracking. "Get your fingers in the gap when I make one. Don't put them anywhere you'd mind losing them." I work the bar until the plates part enough for a hand, and then I haul, and I keep hauling until the doors are open as far as they will go. Whatever is on the other side, I want to see it before it sees us.

Beside me on the left, Sarah stepped up and dropped low into a brace, working her fingers into the seam clear of the steel bar.

"I have purchase on the inner panel. Apply leverage on three." she said in a monotone, even voice.

The tracks screeched as the metal gave, the sliding leaves parting under the strain until they racked back into their wall pockets. The heavy reek of machine oil and hydraulic fluid pushed in off the concrete. Standing at the open threshold while she remained crouched beside the frame, I leaned forward and swept the halogen beam across the landing void.

Beyond the door, the white beam cut into nothing but darkness.

### 6. The beam goes out and finds nothing to stop it, which tells me more than a wall would have. "That's not sub-three." I hold the light steady and read what it gives me — floor level relative to the car, how far out the concrete goes, whether there is a lip to catch a boot. "Sub-three's finished. Tile, painted lines, a sign telling you which way the shelter is." I move the beam along the threshold. "This is bare deck." I step down onto it, one boot, weight through the heel, testing it before I trust it. "Doctor. Stay in the car until I say. If this floor is going anywhere I'd rather it took one of us."

The deck took my weight. Steady pressure pushed back against the boot, continuous and unyielding as I planted firmly on the concrete with my weight braced through the heel, raising the halogen lantern into the dark.

The glare cut across cracked pavement and peeling hazard stripes beneath dead fluorescent fixtures. Buckled emergency blast doors sealed off the upper stairwells nearby, and across the room, a heavy security grate partitioned the passage to the service spine. Through the open doorway to the lower maintenance corridor, nothing showed but black air. Scorched insulation and cold concrete dust cut sharp through the cab's reek of machine oil.

Inside the car, Sarah remained low in a steady crouch. She pulled her hands back from the door track and rested them on her knees, watching the landing outside.

Her words reached me in faint, broken pieces: "...Understood... uncompleted... decommissioned..."

### 7. I put the beam on the blast doors first, because that is the way out and I want to know how bad it is early rather than late. Buckled. Not shut — buckled, the leaves driven out of true and jammed in the frame, and the concrete above them come down in a slab that has been sitting there long enough to grow a skin of dust. Nobody did that tonight. Nobody did that this decade. "Doctor, you can come out. Mind the sill, it's a step down." I turn the light on the grate across the room and hold it there. "Tell me what you know about a sub-level five-A, because as far as the site plan I have been working off for eleven years is concerned, there isn't one."

Sarah rose from her crouch, smoothing the lapels of her white lab coat flat against her shirt. She stepped over the lift's raised sill and brought her dark loafers down onto the cracked concrete landing, coming to a halt at my right in the hard glare of the lamp.

Her voice came in a monotone, even cadence: "Sub-level designators appended with alphabetical suffixes typically denote provisional containment sectors or decommissioned construction shafts expunged from general facility blueprints. I possess no administrative clearance or briefing regarding a five-A."

### 8. "Five-A." I say it to the grate rather than to her. "There's no five-A on any drawing I've ever signed for. There's a four, and there's a six, and I have crawled the ducts between them with a torch in my mouth." The grate is a security type — flat bar, welded lattice, a lock box on the far side where I cannot reach it. I put the light through it and let the beam go as far as it wants. It goes a long way. A corridor, vaulted, running off further than the lamp will carry. "That's a spine. Everything down here will hang off it." I take my hand off the bars. "Doctor, I need you to tell me something and I need the real answer, not the one that's cleared. Was there ever anything on this level, or was it just built and forgotten?"

Sarah turned her gaze across the barred corridor, sliding both hands cleanly into the pockets of her white lab coat. She spoke in a calm voice: "I am giving you the unvarnished answer, not an administrative deflection. I have never been briefed on Sub-level Five-A, and it does not appear in Site-Seventeen's current containment rosters. However, abandoned construction shafts do not receive reinforced partition grates, and dead utility spaces are not fitted with hydraulic blast doors rated for high-yield containment. Something was housed beyond that spine. Whether it was neutralized, transferred, or sealed behind the collapse remains undetermined, but we must proceed under the assumption that this sector is not inert."

### 9. "Then we don't stand here." The lock box is on the far side, which is a design decision somebody made on purpose, and it tells me this grate was meant to keep things in rather than out. Fine. A lattice is only as good as what it is welded to, and what this one is welded to is a frame set into forty-year-old concrete that has had water in it. I go along the frame with the light, low, looking at the bottom corner where the damp always gets in first. Then I set the pry bar behind the bar nearest the corner and put my weight on it, slow, feeling for give rather than trying to snap anything. "Doctor — while I do this. Anything you can tell me about what a blast door on a level like this is rated to hold, I want to hear it. And keep your voice down."

My knee ground hard against the cracked concrete deck, the chill and grit biting through the cloth as I braced my shoulder and leaned my weight into the steel. The flat of the pry bar settled behind the bottom corner bar, holding fast against the rusted seam where the lamp beam cut across the base.

Beside me, Sarah leaned forward slightly toward the partition frame, keeping her hands buried deep in her pockets. Her answer came under her breath, a subdued, monotone murmur: "A reinforced hydraulic bulkhead of this gauge is typically Class-Four. It is rated to withstand sustained kinetic impacts exceeding ten megapascals, or internal high-yield explosive detonation."

She turned her gaze down toward the anchor bolts illuminated along the base of the grate. In a flat, hushed cadence, she added, "The deformation along the frame suggests vertical ceiling collapse rather than internal breach pressure. If the locking pins sheared under load, the door is structurally dead, but the containment seal itself remains compromised."

### 10. The corner gives. Not the lock, not the weld — the concrete, forty years of damp having quietly eaten the anchor, and the bottom bar comes away from its seat with a sound like a spade going into wet gravel. I work it wider, and wider, until there is a gap a person can get through if they are willing to go through it on their belly. "That's what we've got. It isn't dignified." I go through first, bar first, then the lamp, then me, and I come up on the other side and put the light both ways down the spine before I say anything else. "Clear. Come through — and Doctor, when you're up, stay behind my shoulder and don't get in front of the light."

"Dignity is not an operational parameter during containment evacuations." Sarah replied in a deadpan monotone.

She gathered the fabric of her coat tightly against her thighs, dropped to her hands and knees on the rough deck, and slid flat beneath the warped security bar. The hem scraped across the grit as she cleared the threshold, pulling herself through into the corridor.

"I am through. I will maintain position behind your shoulder." she said, her voice dropping to a flat whisper as she gained her feet.

She stood close behind my left shoulder, tucked neatly into the shadow behind the glare while I held the utility lamp leveled down the long, vaulted spine.

### 11. I walk it. Not fast — heel down first, listening between steps, the way you learn to in a plantroom where a running machine will kill the sound of the thing that is about to kill you. The spine is long and the lamp does not reach the end of it. Doors off it, both sides. I put the light on each one as we come to it and read what is written there, because a door on a level like this always says what it was for, even when everything else has been taken away. "I'm looking for two things," I say, low, over my shoulder. "A riser — a service shaft with rungs, they run the full height of the core and they are how the likes of me get about when the lifts are down. And a plant room, because a plant room has a riser next to it more often than not." I keep the beam moving. I do not let it rest anywhere longer than it takes to read.

Behind my left shoulder, Sarah stepped forward in a steady cadence, keeping out of the direct beam while tracking the fixtures mounted along the partition wall. She spoke in a composed, analytical voice: "The portal further along the right partition is stenciled as an auxiliary plant room. If standard Site-Seventeen engineering conventions were maintained during this sector's construction, that chamber offers the highest probability of an intact riser conduit."

### 12. "Agreed. But we're not walking into a plant room blind at the end of a long corridor with one lamp between us." I stop at the plant room door and do not touch it yet. First the frame: I want to know whether it has been opened since it was shut. Dust on the sill, the state of the seal, whether the handle has been turned by anything with hands. "Doctor, listen for a second. Properly listen." I hold still and let the corridor be quiet around us. A plant room with anything alive in it makes a noise — a pump ticking over, water moving, a bearing dry and complaining. A dead one makes none. "Tell me what you hear. I want a second pair of ears on this before I open anything."

The beam cut across the cold steel, tracing the perimeter seal and catching the settled dust on the threshold sill. I kept my fingers clear of the handle and held motionless, turning an ear toward the seam of the bulkhead.

Just behind me, Sarah froze in place. She turned her head slightly, directing her right ear toward the edge of the reinforced frame to listen.

Under her breath, her voice little more than a whisper, she said, "There is no acoustic signature of active mechanical operation. An operational plant room of this scale would produce transformer resonance or forced-air circulation. I also detect no high-frequency whistle around the frame, which indicates there is no active pressure gradient or atmospheric displacement across the seal. Nor is there any audible respiration, shifting mass, or fluid movement. The space behind this bulkhead appears dormant."

### 13. "Dormant's what I wanted to hear." I take the bar to it anyway, because a dead plant room still has a door that has been shut for forty years and swollen in its frame, and there is only one way that opens. The first bite of steel on steel is louder than I want it to be. It goes off down the spine in both directions and comes back off the vaulting a half-second later, and I stop with my hands still on the bar and let it finish coming back before I move again. "That," I say quietly, "is the problem with this whole level. Listen to how far that went." I set the bar again and lean, slower this time, trying to make the door come open in a long groan rather than a bang.

The reverberation was still running out along the ceiling, a sharp, ringing crack that rolled the full length of the vaulted spine and sent a muffled shudder pulsing through the concrete deck. Behind my shoulder, Sarah stood motionless, her head tilted level as she tracked the sound along the overhead conduits into the dark.

In a flat, measured whisper, she said, "A vaulted ferroconcrete corridor with uninsulated cable raceways exhibits an extended reverberation time. If any entity along this conduit relies on acoustic detection, that transient was sufficient to register. Apply continuous leverage to the seal rather than percussive force."

I braced my boots against the deck plates and threw my full weight into the pry bar. Under the sustained leverage, the swollen steel began to give, groaning against rusted internal dogs as the seal finally took the strain.

### 14. The dogs let go one after another, and the leaf comes off its seat with a noise I do not like at all. I do not go in. I put the lamp through the gap first and stand off to the hinge side, and I look before I put any part of me in there. "Doctor. Behind me and to the left, and if I say back, you go back and you do not ask me why until we are somewhere with a door between us." I want three things off this room and I want them fast: a riser, a ladder, or a way up that is not a lift. Anything with rungs. Anything that goes vertical. "And keep listening. I've just told the whole level where we are."

The dogs gave with a shrill metallic shriek, metal grinding on metal as the leaf wrenched outward and broke forty years of rust. I leaned into the hinge side of the frame, holding my ground in the corridor while I thrust the utility lamp through the widening gap. The halogen beam cut into the darkness, sweeping methodically across the machinery hall as I searched the walls and corners for iron rungs or the frame of a riser.

Behind me, Sarah stepped back two paces, squaring herself off my left flank to stay clear of the metal leaf. Her eyes remained fixed down the length of the corridor, her voice coming under her breath in a flat monotone: "Understood. I am positioned on your left flank. I will retreat to the landing gate on your mark without debate. The corridor remains acoustically quiescent."

### 15. "There. That's the one." I go in. Two steps, and I put the lamp up the rungs to see how far they go before I let myself want it. The ladder is bolted to the plant room wall and it goes up into a shaft in the ceiling, and that is the first thing on this level that has been on my side. "Doctor, in and to the left of the door, and pull it to behind you — not shut, I want it a hand's width so we can hear, but I don't want that gap standing open with a lamp behind it." I put my hand flat on the third rung and lean my weight on it, hard, twice. Forty years of damp does things to bolts, and I would rather find that out here than at thirty feet.

A harsh metallic pop snapped through the fixings, followed by the violent rattle of the steel cage shuddering against the concrete deck before the bolts caught.

Behind me, Sarah stepped over the raised metal threshold into the plant room, moving aside to halt against the left wall. She took hold of the reinforced door's edge and drew the heavy leaf toward the frame, checking its swing to leave a narrow gap.

Her voice reached me in muffled fragments: "...corridor... structural... integrity..."

I stayed on my feet, hands locked around the third rung where I had thrown my weight into the mounting. The vibration died out of the steel, leaving only the steady, unyielding pressure of cold iron pressed into my palms.

### 16. "It'll hold. It's not pretty but it'll hold." I go up four rungs and stop, because four rungs is where you find out what the shaft is going to be like without committing to it. The lamp goes up on its strap over my shoulder so both hands are free, and the light goes up the shaft ahead of me and does not find a ceiling. "Doctor — it's a proper riser. It runs. I can't see the top of it from here." Then I stop talking and I hold still on the rungs and I listen up the shaft, and down it, and back out through the gap in the door behind us, and I give it a slow ten count before I say anything else. A shaft carries sound the way a chimney carries smoke, and if there is anything moving on this level, this is where I will hear it first.

The fourth rung pressed unyielding through the soles of my boots, cold iron biting into my palms while the strap weighed steady across my shoulder. Below, Sarah remained braced against the wall beside the entrance. She eased the grip of her fingers along the inner lip of the bulkhead door, holding the gap motionless at four inches. Her head canted slightly, dividing her attention between the corridor seam and the riser, before she shifted her gaze upward along the halogen cone into the vertical gloom of the ceiling aperture.

Then the mountain spoke. A muffled detonation rumbled through the rock, shuddering through the deep strata and buzzing into the ladder rungs in a low seismic tremor.

### 17. I come back down the four rungs, because there is one thing I want to do before we commit to a shaft with no branches in it. "Doctor. Before we go up — I want thirty seconds of you and me both being quiet, and I want you to tell me what you hear off each of these doors. Not what you think it is. What it sounds like." I go along the spine with the lamp low and my hand flat on each leaf as I pass it. Steel tells you things. A door with a room breathing behind it does not feel like a door with forty years of nothing behind it. "Because if there's something running down here, I want to know which side of us it's on before I put us both in a pipe with one way out."

My boots struck the iron plating beneath the ladder with a hollow ring. Clearing the threshold, I stepped through the bulkhead into the vaulted corridor, the utility strap bearing steady pressure against my shoulder as I kept the halogen beam tipped toward the floor. Moving down the spine through the stench of hydraulic fluid, I laid a bare palm against each shut door in passing, testing the chilled steel for any vibration or draft.

Sarah came through the bulkhead behind me, her shape indistinct in the gloom until she halted within arm's reach. "Did the riser present an obstruction, or did you register thermal or acoustic movement through the bulkhead? The plant room remains dormant. If you are checking for temperature differentials, tell me what you feel." she said in an analytical voice.

### 18. I take my hand off the last door and stand still in the middle of the spine and just listen for a while, and I do not like what I have got. "Right. Doctor, here's what I've got and you can tell me if I'm reading it wrong. Four dead doors and one that isn't." I put two fingers against the containment door without pushing on it. "There's plant running behind this one. Not ticking over — labouring. Something's turning in there and it's turning against a blockage, and it has been doing it for forty years after this level was written off and cut from the mains." I let that sit. "Which means it isn't on the mains. Which means somebody left it on a supply that hasn't failed, and you do not do that for a room that's empty." I put the lamp on the ladder, not on the door. "We're not opening it. We go up. But I want you to hear it before we go, because if we get to the top and the hatch is welded, I'm going to have to come back down here and think about that door, and I'd rather we both already know what's behind it is running."

The cold steel pushed back against my fingertips, the unyielding weight of the leaf resting under the contact while the utility lamp's beam stayed turned north, cutting past the corridor partitions toward the open plant room bulkhead and the ladder within.

Sarah stepped forward on my right. She inclined her head toward the seam of the steel door to listen, keeping her distance without making contact with the frame.

Sarah said in a flat, analytical voice: "A dedicated power source functioning independently for four decades indicates autonomous life support or an active failsafe containment mechanism. It is not an abandoned storage depot. I register the mechanical drag against the housing. Your operational appraisal is correct: we do not breach a sealed containment enclosure without diagnostic telemetry. Let us return to the plant room and begin the ascent."

### 19. "Agreed. Go." Back through the bulkhead into the plant room, and this time I pull the leaf shut behind us and lean on it until the dogs seat. It will not lock. It does not need to lock. It needs to be shut. "Doctor, you go first and I'll be under you the whole way. If you slip, you slip into me and I'll take it. If I say stop, you stop with both hands on and both feet on and you do not look down at me." I put my shoulder under the strap, get the lamp angled up past her, and set my boot on the first rung. "And keep your breathing steady and your mouth shut. Not for the noise. Just do it."

The heavy leaf drove home against the rebate, the dogs seating into the frame with a dull, hollow clunk. The reverberation flattened against the rows of dead brine chillers and died away down the dark hall, leaving only the fading sound of my own voice.

I hitched the strap higher onto my shoulder, angled the beam upward into the caged riser shaft, and placed my boot onto the first rung. The cold iron pressed back, steady and unyielding under my weight.

No shadow climbed into the cone of light. Beyond the shut bulkhead, out in the corridor, there had been a flicker of movement—too little of it to make out before the door seated—and now nothing showed through the reinforced plate. No voice came back.

### 20. I get one boot on the second rung before the shape of it lands on me. No hand on my back. No breathing behind my shoulder. Nobody said "understood." I come off that ladder faster than I got on it and I have the dogs up and my shoulder into the leaf before I have finished swearing at myself, and I do not open it wide, I open it a hand's width and put the lamp through low and to the side. "Doctor. Doctor — say something. I've done something stupid and I need you to be exactly where I left you."

The sound of my voice flattened against the iron leaf and ran out into the corridor, dying away down the vaulted concrete. No answer came back. No scrape of soles, no monotone murmur from the dark.

Down on one knee against the cold deck plates, I drove the halogen beam hard through the floor-level gap, sweeping it along the threshold. The glare flared off the rusted steel wedged ajar and cast south down the empty service spine. Behind me, the dead turbines and severed conduits soaked up the backscatter, thinning into half-light. The vast machinery hall held entirely still. Nothing showed in the beam, and nothing moved out on the spine.

### 21. Nothing. Not a sound and not a shape. So I stop being careful about the door and start being careful about her. I get both hands on the leaf and I haul it fully open — wide, all the way back against the stop, so it cannot swing to behind me again — and I go through into the spine with the lamp up at head height where it lights a corridor instead of a floor. "SARAH." Full voice. I don't care what hears it. "Sing out. Anywhere. I'll come to you."

The heavy steel leaf met the wall stop with a dull clang, locking wide open against the frame.

Knuckles rapped twice firmly against the steel bulkhead plate directly above the latch housing. Sarah stood right there within arm's reach, caught in the white glare of the halogen beam—slender and composed in her white lab coat, her violet eyes fixed on me without a blink. In a flat, precise voice, she said, "I am on the exterior side of the bulkhead, Mister Voss. Rotate the internal dog spindle to release the latch. We are expending operational time in the dark."

### 22. I get a hand on her sleeve and I do not let go of it. "That was mine. Entirely mine. I shut a door on you in the dark and I will not do it again, so from here you are within reach of me or you are on a rung above me, and there is no third option." I turn her by the elbow toward the plant room doorway and I put her through it ahead of me, and I leave that leaf standing wide against its stop. "Up. You lead, I'll be a rung under you the whole way. If the rungs go bad, you tell me and you stop — you do not try to be quick about it."

The heavy cloth of her sleeve yielded under my grip, and she stepped across the raised steel threshold into the auxiliary plant room, turning toward the base of the vertical caged ladder. In a flat, measured deadpan voice, she said, "It was an operational lapse. We will not repeat it."

At the base of the ladder, she stopped to inspect the lower mountings before resting both hands against the vertical rails.
