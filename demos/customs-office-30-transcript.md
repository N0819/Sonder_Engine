# customs-office-30: Séverine Moreau / Auditor Dana Rennick

**Scenario.** A rain-soaked customs office on the harbour road, an hour before dawn. One lamp, one desk, one locked ledger. The clerk on duty has been waiting for someone all night and is not sure this is them.

---

Rain ticks against the window. The sound threads through the ajar door at your back, carrying the damp, oily reek of the quay and something older — lamp oil and spent paper — that fills this cramped office like a second atmosphere. The lamp on the scarred desk gutters, and shadows swing across the walls with each flicker. Behind the desk, a clerk perches on a high stool, watching the doorway. Watching you. **> I shake the rain off my coat and step up to the desk. "I'm here about the Kestrel manifest."**

Rain still freckles the floorboards as I stop at the desk. "The Kestrel manifest brings an Auditor out in this rain. What precisely draws your interest in it?" Warm voice. Exact. The clerk's mouth parts, closes — a wet click — and he drags a damp palm down his trouser leg before he turns to the rusted filing cabinet behind him. His fingers hover over a drawer label, not quite touching.

She does not press it; she simply lets it occupy the air, her fingers still flat against the desk edge, her posture unchanged. Then, quieter: "In your experience, are such manifests often found to contain... complications?" Behind the desk, the clerk's hands move across a stack of forms — a shuffle, a pause, the briefest tremor in his fingers before they steady and resume. The oil lamp dips low, throws his shadow long against the filing cabinet, and the room contracts to the smell of salt and damp wool and the warm metal tang of burning wick. Séverine's mouth curves — not quite a smile. "I should hate to think your duties prove overly taxing in this weather."

**> "If anyone asks, you never saw me. Say the ledger was already signed."**

The ledger's already been checked tonight — the seal's been verified. If I say it was signed when it wasn't, that's a falsification. I'd lose my post. I don't know who you are, but I can't lie for you. Not for anyone." Séverine shifts to face me directly. That aristocratic stillness holds — refinement or danger, depending on which instinct you trust. She draws a slow breath, her tone measured, lightly inquisitive: "Auditor, it seems your business with the ledger this evening requires a measure of discretion. One cannot help but wonder what aspect of the Kestrel manifest warrants instructing the clerk to deny your presence. Does the impending seal at midnight anticipate complications best kept from the official record? Or does it reflect a more personal interest that might complicate your duties?"

The number passes between us low enough that the lamp's sputter covers it — four digits, a place to keep them. The clerk's throat works. His fingers drift toward the ledger's chain, then away, and his eyes slide to the woman before they come back to me, wide and wet. He doesn't write anything down. Séverine Moreau's head tilts that precise half-inch, her gaze unblinking, and whatever she reads in the clerk's posture is enough. "Auditor, the clerk's reluctance appears quite genuine. One must ask what the Kestrel manifest carries that justifies placing him in such an unenviable position." The clerk flinches at *genuine* — a small, involuntary contraction, his stool creaking under the shift. The salt-and-lamp-oil air sits thick between us. Séverine's hands rest where they've been resting, her composure a thing with no seams in it, and the rain keeps its steady tick through the ajar door at my back.

**> I glance at the door, then lower my voice. "How long have you worked here?"**

My eyes cut to the harbour road door — rain-dark, standing open — and I lean toward the clerk. The data-slate drops a fraction in my hand. The question comes at a whisper, pitched beneath the lamp's sputter. He goes still on the stool. His knuckles don't loosen on the desk edge. He doesn't answer. Séverine's nostrils flare once — a single controlled inhale — her gaze fixed on the space between the clerk and me. Her voice lands low and precise in the damp air. "Auditor, that number appears to carry considerable weight given how it tests the clerk's resolve. Tell me, does four-seven-three-one pertain to cargo that cannot survive official scrutiny?"

**> "Who told you to wait for me tonight?"**

The question lands in the room and stays there. Séverine Moreau's mouth lifts at the corners — barely, a twitch of muscle more than expression — and her head turns toward the open doorway behind me. Salt, damp wool, lamp oil — and underneath it all, the sharp bite of something that isn't either of those things.

**> "Everything I said before tonight was someone else's script."**

**> "My name is Wren. That much is true, and it's the only thing that is."**
