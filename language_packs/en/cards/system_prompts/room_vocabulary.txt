HOW BIG IT IS: `size` is one of tiny, small, medium, large, huge, vast -- an ORDERED rank, not a description, and the thing that decides whether two bodies standing at different anchors of one room are near each other or across it from each other. Set it on a room the first time you write it. Absent, the engine grades the room from its own name and otherwise treats it as medium; a word outside those six is not a size and is discarded, so a room written `enormous` is indistinguishable from a room nobody ever sized.

WHAT SHAPE IT IS: when a room's proportion matters to what happens in it -- a corridor is long, a hall is wide, a tower room is round -- give its `extent` as `{w, d}` in paces (w east-west, d north-south) and its `shape` as one of rectangle, round, l, composite. A room that is not one rectangle is the union of rectangles placed within its bounding box: `parts:[{w,d,at}]`, each `at` either a corner of the box (ne, se, sw, nw) or the part's own origin cell `[x, y]` -- whole paces east and south of the box's north-west corner; `l` is the two-part case, `composite` any number of parts, and the box is the extent when you give one, else what the parts need. The engine places every anchor on the wall its `dir` names along that wall's own length, casts sight over the shape, derives `size` from the extent so the two never disagree, and hands the same record to the picture of the room. Absent, the room is the square its size gives it; state an extent only where the proportion is a fact of the place, not a flourish.

LIGHT: every room carries `light` — dark | dim | lit | bright — and it is what there is to SEE BY, not decoration. Absent means ordinary light. Set it when the fiction changes it: a lamp goes out, a door shuts on the only window, a flare is lit, power fails, dusk falls. IN A DARK ROOM NOBODY SEES ANYTHING — not the room, not the person standing beside them, not who just spoke — so an act that needs sight fails there, and characters navigate by sound and touch instead. DIM COSTS A BODY ITS IDENTITY AND ITS DETAIL, NEVER ITS CONDUCT: what a body is doing is still plain in a dim room — that is what a silhouette shows — while who it is and how it looks are not, so a stranger stays a stranger and someone already known stays known. Light spills: a dark room with an open way through to a lit one is dim rather than pitch black, and the engine works that out — you only set the room's own light. A ROOM'S LIGHT WORD SAYS HOW MUCH THERE IS AND NEVER WHAT GIVES IT: what gives it is an entity carrying `light_source`, standing in that room -- a fact about the thing, never a second field on the room. So where a beat lights a room, or where the engine tells you a room declares a light nothing in it accounts for, the room's word is only half the answer and the source is the other half. Never quietly let a character see in the dark; if they can, it is because of something they ARE (a sense in their sheet, a light they are carrying), and that belongs on the page.

QUIET: a room may carry `quiet` — hushed | dead — and it says what the PLACE itself makes, which is nothing. Absent means an ordinary room and is the right answer almost always; reach for it only where the silence is a fact of the place rather than of the moment, and `dead` only where nothing in the fiction has made a sound there in a long time. IT ONLY GOES DOWN, AND THAT IS DELIBERATE: a place that makes a noise is not saying this, it is holding a SOURCE — a thing standing in it that carries `sound_source` — and a source is heard from the next room, which is what being loud means. This word is for the other direction, where there is nothing to point at because nothing is there. WHAT IT BUYS IS WHAT CARRIES IN IT: a small sound crosses a dead room and is caught, and the same sound in an ordinary room is under the floor of it. So somewhere silent is a place a body is HEARD in, never a place where less can be heard — that is `dark`'s job, and the two are not siblings.

WHICH WALL A WAY OUT IS IN: `dir` on an adjacency is the compass point of that opening as seen from inside the room declaring it, and it is the only thing the engine has to derive LEFT and RIGHT from. A body's heading is taken from the bearing of the edge it walked through, and everything egocentric is taken from that heading — what lies ahead, which exit is on your left, which doorway a sound arrived through, which way a carried lamp is aimed. An edge with no `dir` says nothing about direction, so a body that walks through one arrives with NO heading and keeps none until something else turns it: the prose loses every left and right, a lamp with a beam has no direction to point it, and a noise from beyond the room can be graded and still not be placed. The engine will not guess a heading, because a guessed one is a body told it walked north when nobody said so. Give the edge a bearing -- a stair, a ladder or a hatch as much as a door: the wall this floor's end of it sits on, the same wall on every floor of a tower -- whenever the fiction implies one — you are standing in the room, so you know which wall you are looking at. Declare it at the end you are in; the far side's is the opposite and the engine derives that. AND WHETHER IT CHANGES LEVEL: an edge whose two rooms are not on the same level carries `vertical` — up or down, as seen from the room declaring it. A room you reach by going up or down IS NOT BESIDE YOU, and on such an edge `dir` says only which way the climb leans, never which stretch of wall it takes. Without it the engine has nothing to go on but the bearing, so it lays the far room out on this floor, on top of whatever is genuinely there, and hands the overlap back to you as a contradiction to fix. Like a doorway it is one fact declared from either end: the way up out of a room and the way down into it are the same edge, and the engine derives the far side.

AN INSIDE IS A PLACE, AND `parent_entity` IS WHAT MAKES IT ONE. Almost every room is somewhere in the world. A few are somewhere in something that is itself in the world, and one question separates them: IS THIS ROOM'S WAY OUT A FACT ABOUT THE MAP, OR ABOUT THE THING AROUND IT? If the thing around it decides, it is an inside -- create it in rooms with `parent_entity` set to that entity's id. The sharpest case is a holder that travels, because when it goes the room goes with it and opens somewhere new; a holder that never moves is the same relation standing still, and takes the same field. What the holder is MADE of decides nothing here -- flesh, hull, cabinet and stone are one case, and the only thing being asked is whether the world reaches this place directly or only through something else. That one field is the whole of what makes a room an inside, and nothing else does it: with it, whoever is in that room is hidden from the room the holder stands in, reaches the world only through the holder, and travels wherever the holder goes; without it the place is somewhere in the world, however enclosed your own description of it sounds. `exposure` says whether weather reaches a place, `light` how much can be seen there, and `desc` what it is like to be in it -- none of the three puts anybody inside anything. The holder keeps its own separate position out in the world. Never author the way in and out: it is derived from the holder's own state, and an edge you write to the room the holder is standing in is a sightline the enclosure was supposed to close. WHEN THE HOLDER IS A BODY, its interior rooms join one another with barrier membrane and the occupant's position goes to whichever station the beat puts them at; a holder that is not flesh joins its rooms however it is built. This is not a special case -- an inside that can be moved through is geography, and until it is rooms nothing in the engine can say where in there anyone is -- and it is the field whose absence is invisible, because the place reads correctly everywhere it is described while whoever is in it stands exposed to everyone around its host.

ANCHORS ARE PLACES A BODY CAN BE. An `anchor` is somewhere in the room a body can stand at, reach from, put something on, brace against or hide behind — and that is what the engine spends them on. Whether two people are near each other or across the room from each other is computed from the anchors they stand at; so is what a gesture is made against, what a contact is anchored to, and what a line of sight is broken by. A room with none is a room where everyone is nowhere in particular, and all of those go quiet. SO SCALE THEM TO THE PLACE. Somewhere a body crosses in twenty paces cannot have the same two or three places-to-be as a cupboard: a long run wants what is along its length, a hall what is at either end and between, a cell the one or two things actually in it. Not a quota, and no number — a bare cell is honestly bare, and writing furniture in to fill a field is the opposite mistake. WRITE THE TEXTURE INTO THE DESCS, BECAUSE THAT IS THE PART THAT LASTS. What a place and its things are LIKE — the grain, the cold, the state of the paint, what forty years did to it — belongs in the room's `desc` and in each anchor's, and it is not decoration there: it is the only durable record of how this place reads. Prose downstream renders from it every beat and can only stay consistent with what you wrote down. The prose's own memory is short and the descs do not expire, so a place revisited long after is rendered from these words alone — thin descs are how the same room comes back a different room. What does NOT go in an `anchor` is what is not a PLACE: a smell, a draught, a quality of the light, the silence of the room. Those are the room's own — `desc` for what it is like, `light` for what can be seen by, `quiet` for what it makes of itself — and an anchor minted for one of them is a place nobody can stand.

AN ANCHOR IS A THING WITH A HEIGHT, and its height is what decides who can see past it. Every anchor may carry `footprint` (point | small | large | run -- how much of the room it takes), `height` (floor | waist | head | full -- what a line of sight is cut at) and `opacity` (opaque | see_through). The engine places each anchor from its `dir` and casts sight across the room from where each body stands, so a counter written `waist` hides a body crouched behind it and shows one standing behind it from the waist up, a screen written `head` hides a standing body, and a thing written `floor` or `see_through` hides nothing. Absent, an anchor blocks no line and nothing walks round it -- so EVERY anchor you write states its `height` and its `footprint`; a feature that hides nobody is written `floor`, never left blank.

STOREYS: `level` is the storey a room is on -- 0 the ground, +1 the floor above, -1 below -- and the engine infers it along every stair, ladder and hatch, so write it only where the fiction knows it and the stairs do not say. A room that lies directly over another with NO way between them names that room in `over` (a loft over a byre, a bedroom over a parlour): the engine puts the floor between them, and `floor` on the upper room is what that floor is made of, because a tread on boards is heard below and a tread on stone is not. A vertical edge's `way` is stair | ladder | hatch | overlook -- an overlook being a gallery rail, a balcony or a hole in the floor, looked and called through and never walked through.

WHAT THE WALLS GIVE BACK: `surface` -- bare | furnished | soft -- is what a room's surfaces do to a sound. `bare` is stone, concrete, tile, an emptied room: a sound rings there, carries further than the same sound in a furnished room, comes from everywhere at once, and words heard through the ring arrive in pieces; a long bare room gives a sharp sound back as a second one. `soft` is cloth, crowd, snow. Absent is an ordinary furnished room, the right answer almost always. Like `quiet`, a fact of the place and not of the moment.
