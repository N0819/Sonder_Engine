The Shrine of the Golden Fox

Hidden high on the mist-wrapped slopes of a remote mountain in Kyoto Prefecture stands a shrine of immeasurable age: the dwelling of Tamamo-no-Mae, the nine-tailed fox who has guarded the dimensional seal for over two millennia. It is built as a Heian-period dwelling-shrine of hinoki timber under a deep tiled roof, and it faces south, down the mountain toward the torii. It has two full storeys and a small room under the roof, and beneath them a basement carved into the living mountain.

THE GROUNDS
A karesansui garden of raked gravel, moss-wrapped granite boulders and chipped stone lanterns surrounds the shrine on every side. A vermillion torii of ancient hinoki stands at the southern edge of the grounds; just inside it two worn stone kitsune guardians flank the approach. From the torii the Lantern Path runs straight north to the shrine's front door, lined on both sides by weathered stone lanterns. Near the front door, on the east side of the path, sits an ancient stone well with a wooden bucket and hemp rope. Behind the shrine, on its north side, rises a camphor tree so massive several people can shelter beneath it, bound with a shimenawa rope. The latrine is a separate small structure behind the shrine's west end, reached along a short roofed walkway from the back door of the washroom.

GROUND FLOOR
The front door opens from the Lantern Path into the GENKAN, a small entry hall with an earthen floor in the middle of the south front: a step up marks the strict boundary where footwear is forbidden. A shoe rack stands against its east wall, and a wooden tablet in archaic kanji hangs above the step: 'That which stains the world stops here. Purify your presence.'

The step leads north into the MAIN HALL (O-HEYA), a twelve-mat tatami room at the heart of the house, its ceiling held by vermillion lacquered pillars. An engawa veranda runs along its east side behind sliding shoji, open to the garden. In the middle of the room is the sitting area: a low lacquered chabudai table ringed with silk zabuton cushions, a hibachi brazier beside it, painted screens standing behind. The tokonoma alcove is set into the middle of the north wall, the place of honour, with its hanging scroll and ikebana vase. The oshiire, deep wall closets with sliding doors, fill the west wall; inside are wooden chests of kimono and hidden compartments of old talismans and letters.

In the north wall, east of the tokonoma, a shoji door opens onto the BOX STAIRCASE: a steep straight flight of dark wood rising along the north wall to the floor above, with drawers built into the space beneath its steps. West of the tokonoma, a second shoji door opens onto STEEP STONE STAIRS that descend straight into the mountain.

East of the genkan and the main hall lies the KITCHEN (DAIDOKORO), a six-mat room on the service side of the house with an earthen-floored half where the clay-and-stone kamado stove stands against the east wall. Iron pots hang from wooden racks; ceramic jars of rice, dried vegetables, preserved fruits and fermented goods line the north wall; a stone washing basin stands by the kitchen's back door, which opens onto the path to the well.

West of the main hall lies the WASHROOM (OFUROYA): a deep cedar ofuro tub large enough for nine tails, heated by a wood-fired stove beneath, with wooden stools and basins for washing before the soak and ceramic jars of herbal soaps on a shelf. Its back door opens north onto the roofed walkway to the latrine.

SECOND FLOOR
The box staircase arrives at the north end of the HALLWAY, a narrow wooden corridor running north to south across the middle of the upper floor, which stands over the main hall. Windows line its west wall. On its east side two shoji doors open, in order from the north: the MEDITATION ROOM, a four-and-a-half-mat room of bare polished wood with a single window facing the forest to the east, a lone zabuton in the middle facing a small altar with a bronze mirror and an offering bowl; then HINAMI'S ROOM, a six-mat tatami room with a window facing the moonrise to the east. Hinami's room holds a futon heaped with conflicting blankets and a massive trunk at its foot, a low wooden shelf of small gods along the north wall (a terrarium, ceramic figurines, a cracked smartphone and solar charger, a polaroid camera, a box of photographs, a battered radio, convenience-store candies, a laptop), paper lanterns hanging from the ceiling, woven textiles, woodblock prints and a 1920s circus poster on the walls, and a glass wind chime in the window.

At the south end of the hallway, a shoji door opens onto a narrow, steep straight stair rising to the roof room.

TOP FLOOR
TAMAMO'S ROOST is the room under the peaked roof, smaller than the floor below because of the roofline, the most private space in the shrine. In its centre is the nest: a massive arrangement of futons, silk comforters and oversized pillows built for nine tails, the outer comforter embroidered in gold with running foxes and chrysanthemums. A small altar against the north wall holds memorial tablets with fresh flowers and incense; a low writing desk stands beneath the window in the south gable, with a calligraphy set, brushes, an inkstone and paper; wooden boxes of finished writings line the east wall; an ancient shamisen rests near the window.

BASEMENT
The stone stairs descend into the BASEMENT, carved directly into bedrock under the north half of the shrine, warm and damp, lit by oil lamps in wall niches that burn without fuel. Shelves hold ceramic jars and sake barrels; rope-bound boxes of festival equipment are stacked by the walls; mushrooms grow on inoculated logs; a scarred wooden workbench stands against the east wall. At the far north end of the basement is a heavy wood door banded in black iron and covered in shifting ofuda wards: it leads into the Seal Cavern, a vast steaming cavern of hot spring pools with the Dimensional Seal at its rear -- a threshold to the divine realm.
